THE NAV BAY -- where the boards go, and whether they go
============================================================================================

  station               318 -> 445 mm from the nose tip
  bay length            127.0 mm (1.60 cal)
  bore                  74.8 mm
  sled width            56.2 mm (80% of the bore)
  usable height         42.1 mm, NOT 74.8

  THE LAST LINE IS THE WHOLE MODELLING POINT. A flat sled in a round tube can only
  use the rectangle inscribed in the circle, and nothing goes in the corners it
  cannot reach. Add the component volumes up and the bay looks half empty --
  94 cm3 of boards in 558 cm3 of tube. The binding quantity is FOOTPRINT ON
  TWO FACES, and the bay's length is what that footprint has to fit into.

THE NOSE -- where the tracker and the radio went, Aug 2026
============================================================================================

  A NOSE IS THE ONE VOLUME IN THIS VEHICLE WHOSE WIDTH IS A FUNCTION OF STATION.
  Every bay so far could be checked with one diameter. Here the sled is only as
  wide as its NARROWEST end allows -- its forward end -- so the answer is a band,
  and the sled wants to sit as far aft as it can where the cone is fullest.

     station        OD   usable ID
  ----------------------------------
       150 mm    57.5 mm      52.9 mm
       200 mm    68.7 mm      64.1 mm
       250 mm    75.9 mm      71.3 mm
       280 mm    78.3 mm      73.7 mm
       318 mm    79.4 mm      74.8 mm

  FITS   nose stack        35.4 mm sled at station 282-318 mm, 59.1 mm wide  2 parts, aft of the ballast
  centroid at station 300 mm, which is where the 105 g
  now sits instead of 381 mm.

  Behind the ballast, not in front of it: the 100 g washer stack is at 191 mm on a
  threaded rod and these two boards go on the same rod, 20 mm clear of it. Both are
  RF parts and neither needs a short wire to the flight computer, so the nose is
  the right place on RF grounds independently of the packing -- the shoulder region
  is where a fibreglass airframe stops shielding an antenna.

  IT FITS ON THE AREAL MODEL, AND THAT IS STILL NOT A REASON TO CHANGE GEOMETRY EITHER WAY.

  This line used to read 'STILL SHORT BY 14 mm' as a hardcoded string while the
  computed verdict three lines above already said FITS. It was stale from before
  D7 closed, and a report that types its own conclusion is not reporting one.
  It is derived now. (docs/04 section 5 carried the same stale figure in two more places; both fixed.)

  AND THE AREAL ANSWER IS NOT THE WHOLE ANSWER. Placing the same stack as real
  rectangles on two real faces -- which this model does not do, by design -- says
  the boards fit and the 80 g wiring loom does not, on a 56.16 mm plate. See
  design/sled.py and `python scripts/sled_report.py`. Nothing there resizes the
  bay either.

  Correction 5 is the precedent and it is exact: the recovery bay was declared
  11 mm short on a number built from two multiplied estimates, the airframe was
  lengthened on the strength of it, and the shortfall turned out not to exist. Every
  envelope above is that same kind of estimate -- no part here has been chosen.

  The move was worth 51 mm of the 47 mm gap. What is left:

  1. CLOSE D7 AND MEASURE. Six datasheets replace six guesses, and 14 mm is well
     inside what a 70% packing efficiency guess is worth -- at 0.80 the stack fits
     with +16.5 cm2 to spare. This is the only thing that settles it, and it is
     ten minutes of work per part.
  2. THE NOSE SHOULDER IS UNMODELLED AND IT IS BIGGER THAN 14 mm -- see below.
  3. ONE END CLOSURE, NOT TWO. The bay's aft face is shared with the canard
     module's forward face. If that plate already exists as the module's structure,
     that is 12 mm back and the shortfall is gone.
  4. LENGTHEN THE NAV BAY. 1.6 -> 2.0 cal is +32 mm and it touches every number in
     the vehicle. Last for that reason, not first.

============================================================================================
THE SHOULDER QUESTION -- SETTLED, and it went the good way
============================================================================================

  This report used to end by saying the nose shoulder was unmodelled and worth more
  than the shortfall: a 1 caliber shoulder on a 127 mm bay, and nobody had decided
  which tube's length it spent. The two readings were 79 mm apart.

  BOTH READINGS WERE WRONG, because the question had a false premise. A shoulder is
  a TUBE. So is a coupler. What is inside it is still bay. Neither costs LENGTH --
  what each costs is local DIAMETER over its span:

      coupler / shoulder bore = 74.8 - 2 x 2.3 = 70.2 mm

  What costs length is a BULKHEAD, and bulkheads were already counted. So:

    nav bay           127.0 mm tube ->  115.0 mm usable, bore 74.8 mm (70.2 over 127 mm)
    canard module     142.9 mm tube ->  130.9 mm usable, bore 74.8 mm (70.2 over 159 mm)
    recovery bay      357.3 mm tube ->  357.3 mm usable, bore 74.8 mm (70.2 over 159 mm)
    booster           416.3 mm tube ->  404.3 mm usable, bore 74.8 mm (70.2 over 79 mm)

  The nav bay gains 12 mm of length (one bulkhead, not two -- the
  forward closure belongs to the nose module) and loses 3.6 mm of sled width to
  the shoulder bore. Net, the shortfall goes 14 -> 9 mm.

  The framing is what had been wrong, not any number. "Which bay does the
  shoulder's length come out of" is a complete-sounding question whose two answers
  were both false, which is correction 28 one joint further forward.

============================================================================================
THE NOSE AS A SWAPPABLE MODULE -- and what a future payload may weigh
============================================================================================

  Putting the tracker and the radio in the nose was a packing fix. Keeping them
  there on purpose is a different decision: the nose becomes the INSTRUMENTATION
  MODULE, and the point of one is that it comes off. After GV-2 has measured the
  control derivatives, the same nose can carry a payload instead.

  That needs three things to be true, and the third is the one nobody expects:

    telemetry radio            one connector to the flight computer -- flight data downlink
    GPS tracker, independent   none; own battery, own antenna, independent by design

    -> ONE electrical interface for the whole module. A payload that needs nothing
       leaves it unmated. The tracker having its own battery is not a nicety; an
       independent tracker sharing the flight computer's battery is not independent.

    -> ITS MASS IS PART OF THE STABILITY SOLUTION. 105 g at station 300 mm sits
       forward of the 780 mm CG, so it is doing the same job as the nose
       ballast. Swap it for something heavier and the margin moves:

    nose payload   SM min   SM max   verdict
  --------------------------------------------------
            0 g     2.18     2.66   ok
           50 g     2.24     2.72   ok
          105 g     2.30     2.77   ok  <- instrumentation
          200 g     2.39     2.87   ok
          300 g     2.49     2.97   ok
          500 g     2.68     3.16   SM over 3.0 -- over-stable

  SO: ANYTHING UP TO ABOUT 300 g GOES IN WITH NO OTHER CHANGE, and past that the
  vehicle turns over-stable rather than unstable -- it weathercocks and gives up
  crossrange, which is a performance loss and not a safety one. The nose ballast is
  the trim knob either way: 100 g at station 191 mm with provision for 300 g
  (docs/00 D10), and it was always meant to be set after weighing the real vehicle.

  Room: 469 cm3 between station 200 mm (clear of the ballast) and the
  nose base, of which the instrumentation sled uses 155 cm3 at 282-318 mm.
  A payload gets the lot once the tracker and radio come out.

  THE ONE DRAWBACK, stated rather than buried: the GNSS antenna stays in the NAV
  BAY, at the forward end of its sled, under the shoulder -- configure.py puts the
  nav bay forward for exactly that reason. A dense payload sitting directly ahead of
  it is between that antenna and the sky. Nothing here models RF; if the payload is
  metallic, check the fix before the flight rather than after it.
