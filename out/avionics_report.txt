THE NAV BAY -- where the boards go, and whether they go
============================================================================================

  station               318 -> 445 mm from the nose tip
  bay length            127.0 mm (1.60 cal)
  bore                  74.8 mm
  sled width            59.8 mm (80% of the bore)
  usable height         44.9 mm, NOT 74.8

  THE LAST LINE IS THE WHOLE MODELLING POINT. A flat sled in a round tube can only
  use the rectangle inscribed in the circle, and nothing goes in the corners it
  cannot reach. Add the component volumes up and the bay looks half empty --
  109 cm3 of boards in 558 cm3 of tube. The binding quantity is FOOTPRINT ON
  TWO FACES, and the bay's length is what that footprint has to fit into.

============================================================================================
THE NOSE -- where the tracker and the radio went, Aug 2026
============================================================================================

  A NOSE IS THE ONE VOLUME IN THIS VEHICLE WHOSE WIDTH IS A FUNCTION OF STATION.
  Every bay so far could be checked with one diameter. Here the sled is only as
  wide as its NARROWEST end allows -- its forward end -- so the answer is a band,
  and the sled wants to sit as far aft as it can where the cone is fullest.

     station        OD   usable ID
  ----------------------------------
       150 mm    57.5 mm      52.9 mm
       200 mm    68.7 mm      64.1 mm
       250 mm    75.9 mm      71.3 mm
       280 mm    78.3 mm      73.7 mm
       318 mm    79.4 mm      74.8 mm

  FITS   nose stack        35.4 mm sled at station 282-318 mm, 59.1 mm wide  2 parts, aft of the ballast
  centroid at station 300 mm, which is where the 105 g
  now sits instead of 381 mm.

  Behind the ballast, not in front of it: the 100 g washer stack is at 191 mm on a
  threaded rod and these two boards go on the same rod, 20 mm clear of it. Both are
  RF parts and neither needs a short wire to the flight computer, so the nose is
  the right place on RF grounds independently of the packing -- the shoulder region
  is where a fibreglass airframe stops shielding an antenna.

============================================================================================
THE VERDICT, ON ESTIMATED ENVELOPES
============================================================================================

  NO FIT nav bay          need 116.8 mm, have 103.0 mm ( -13.8 mm)  short by 14 mm
  NO FIT nav bay          need 151.7 mm, have 103.0 mm ( -48.7 mm)  short by 49 mm      before the move

  available = 127 mm of bay less two 12 mm end closures = 103 mm of sled

  STILL SHORT BY 14 mm, AND STILL NOT YET A REASON TO CHANGE GEOMETRY.

  Correction 5 is the precedent and it is exact: the recovery bay was declared
  11 mm short on a number built from two multiplied estimates, the airframe was
  lengthened on the strength of it, and the shortfall turned out not to exist. Every
  envelope above is that same kind of estimate -- no part here has been chosen.

  The move was worth 35 mm of the 49 mm gap. What is left:

  1. CLOSE D7 AND MEASURE. Six datasheets replace six guesses, and 14 mm is well
     inside what a 70% packing efficiency guess is worth -- at 0.80 the stack fits
     with +0.8 cm2 to spare. This is the only thing that settles it, and it is
     ten minutes of work per part.
  2. THE NOSE SHOULDER IS UNMODELLED AND IT IS BIGGER THAN 14 mm -- see below.
  3. ONE END CLOSURE, NOT TWO. The bay's aft face is shared with the canard
     module's forward face. If that plate already exists as the module's structure,
     that is 12 mm back and the shortfall is gone.
  4. LENGTHEN THE NAV BAY. 1.6 -> 2.0 cal is +32 mm and it touches every number in
     the vehicle. Last for that reason, not first.

============================================================================================
THE THING THIS CHECK CANNOT SEE, AND IT IS LARGER THAN THE SHORTFALL
============================================================================================

  THE NOSE SHOULDER IS NOT IN THIS MODEL AT ALL. `docs/04` carries a 1 caliber
  (79 mm) shoulder on a 127 mm nav bay, and a shoulder inserts INTO the
  forward end of the tube it joins. Which tube's length it spends is a question
  nobody has answered, and the two answers are 79 mm apart:

    * if the sled has to sit AFT of the shoulder, the bay loses 79 mm rather than
      the 12 mm this check charges, and it is short by ~80 mm, not 14;
    * if the sled runs UP INSIDE the shoulder -- which is how many high-power
      av-bays are actually built -- the bay GAINS most of that 79 mm and fits with
      room to spare.

  This check assumes neither. It charges a plain end closure at both ends, which is
  the arrangement nobody has drawn. **The 14 mm shortfall is the smallest open
  question about this bay, not the largest one.** Settle the shoulder before
  spending an afternoon on the packing efficiency.

WHAT THE STACK MEANS FOR VENTING
============================================================================================

  free air in the bay   344 cm3 (geometric less the boards)
  static ports          3 x dia 3.2 mm, 24.1 mm2
  lag, 177 m/s up       0.012 m
  lag, 19 m/s down      0.0001 m

  This is the ONLY volume the altimeter senses. The canard module vents through
  its own wall and the wiring pass-through between them is potted solid -- see
  design/venting.py, and note that seal.py argued the opposite first.
